# Is It a Loan? How States Regulate Earned Wage Access and Interchange on Tax and Tips

How states classify earned wage access, what their laws require, how a federal bill would change what states can decide, and how Illinois and Colorado handled card interchange fees on sales tax and tips. Current as of October 12, 2026.

**Interactive version:** [jphoebus.github.io/state-payments-policy](https://jphoebus.github.io/state-payments-policy/)

## Why this comparison

My other projects ask how states decide what to do when a new industry outgrows the existing rules, mostly in energy and data centers. This one applies the same method to payments. Earned wage access raises a classification question (is it a loan?), and interchange on tax and tips raises a who-pays question, the same one behind [large-load tariffs](https://jphoebus.github.io/large-load-tariffs/), in a different market.

## Headline findings

- **Earned wage access:** eleven states have written laws, and the first decision in each, whether the product is a loan, shapes the rest. Nine treat it as something other than a loan and rely mainly on disclosure; most also require a free option. Maryland and Connecticut treat it as credit and cap fees. A federal bill now on the House calendar (H.R. 9330) would bar states from treating compliant products as credit.
- **Interchange on tax and tips:** federal preemption is setting the outer limit of what states can do. Illinois’ law has been enjoined for national banks, federal credit unions, federal savings associations, out-of-state state banks, and card networks, and its start date has moved twice, now to July 1, 2027. Colorado’s governor cited that litigation when he vetoed a similar bill.

## The eleven states

Nevada, Missouri, Wisconsin, Kansas, South Carolina, Utah, Arkansas, Indiana, Louisiana (not a loan); Maryland and Connecticut (credit). Pennsylvania, Virginia, Ohio, and Georgia have no law; Ohio has two bills pending in committee.

## What stands out

- **Classification comes first.** Most of the nine that say it is not a loan regulate through registration or licensing, disclosure, and a free option. The credit states regulate through lending licenses and fee caps.
- **Fee caps are rare; free options are common.** Only Maryland, Connecticut, and Indiana cap consumer fees. Ten of eleven laws require a no-cost option.
- **Tips are allowed almost everywhere, with conditions.** Maryland moved from a zero-default rule to a ban, effective Oct. 1, 2026.
- **2026 was a year of amendments.** No new state enacted a law; Missouri, Maryland, and Utah amended theirs.
- **On interchange, the courts are setting the pace.** Illinois remains the only state with an enacted law. Colorado’s governor cited the case directly, and most other 2026 bills stopped in committee.

## What to watch

- Fall 2026: House floor action on H.R. 9330; any Senate companion.
- Fall 2026: Whether Illinois appeals the June and September injunctions (not confirmed).
- December 31, 2026: Ohio’s 136th General Assembly ends, with SB 117 and HB 152 in committee.
- January 2027: New sessions, including whether interchange bills held for study return.
- March 1, 2027: First annual earned wage access reports due in Louisiana.
- July 1, 2027: Illinois’ interchange law takes effect for institutions not covered by the injunction.
- December 31, 2029: Nevada’s law expires unless extended.

The question I expect to shape the next cycle: whether Congress settles the loan question for every state, or leaves it with states that have already answered it in different ways.

## About the data

- [earned-wage-access.csv](earned-wage-access.csv): one row per state with an enacted law (law, effective date, classification, license or registration, fee limits, tips, free option, treatment of employer-integrated and direct-to-consumer products, sources), plus rows for Pennsylvania, Virginia, Ohio, and Georgia.
- [interchange-tax-tips.csv](interchange-tax-tips.csv): Illinois, Colorado, and other 2026 bills on interchange for the tax and tip portions of a transaction.

Facts are drawn from statutes, enrolled bills, legislative records, regulator pages, court orders, the Federal Register, and congressional reports. Where an official site blocked automated access, the file links a text copy of the same document and says so. Where a detail could not be confirmed, the file says so rather than guessing. This comparison is for policy analysis and is not legal or regulatory advice.

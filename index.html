Is it a loan? How states set payments rules

How states classify earned wage access, what their laws require, how a federal bill would change what states can decide, and how Illinois and Colorado handled card interchange fees on sales tax and tips. Current as of October 12, 2026.

Earned wage access: eleven states have written laws, and the first decision in each, whether the product is a loan, shapes the rest. Nine treat it as something other than a loan and rely mainly on disclosure; most also require a free option. Maryland and Connecticut treat it as credit and cap fees. A federal bill now on the House calendar would bar states from treating compliant products as credit.

Interchange on tax and tips: federal preemption is setting the outer limit of what states can do. Illinois’ law has been enjoined for national banks, federal credit unions, federal savings associations, out-of-state state banks, and card networks, and its start date has moved twice. Colorado’s governor cited that litigation when he vetoed a similar bill.

The federal layer

CFPB advisory opinion, Dec. 23, 2025

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau concluded that “Covered EWA is not credit” under the Truth in Lending Act and Regulation Z. Coverage turns on how the product works, not on whether an employer is involved: the amount is limited to wages already earned, based on payroll data, recovered through a payroll deduction, with no recourse against the worker and no credit check. In the normal course, expedited-delivery fees and voluntary tips are not finance charges. The opinion withdrew the CFPB’s 2024 proposal, which would have treated many of these products as credit.

H.R. 9330, the Earned Wage Access Consumer Protection Act

Introduced June 18, 2026 by Rep. Bryan Steil and ordered reported 29–22 by House Financial Services on June 30. Reported Sept. 1 (H. Rept. 119-790) and placed on the Union Calendar; no floor action as of this update.

Section 2(b)(2) would bar any state from treating compliant earned wage access as “credit, a loan, debt, or a substantially similar product or service,” or from laws that “prevent or significantly interfere with” it. Section 2(b)(3) preserves state “laws of general applicability,” such as fraud and unfair or deceptive practices laws. The committee report says the savings clause does not preempt state fee limits unless a cap is so onerous that it would “prevent or significantly interfere with” the service.

During markup, amendments to cap fees and tips at $10 a month and to preserve state laws that give consumers greater protection each failed 23–28. A broader CFPB bill, H.R. 10184, carries a parallel earned wage access section.

What it would mean for states

The bill would settle the classification question for every state at once. The nine states that treat it as something other than a loan already classify it the same way. Maryland and Connecticut chose the credit approach, which the bill would bar for compliant products; whether their fee caps survive would turn on the “significantly interfere” standard.

The committee report says the bill “provides a federal framework” because the CFPB “has answered this question differently” over time and, without a federal standard, “states are left to devise their own frameworks.”

On June 30, 2026, 15 attorneys general, including Maryland’s, wrote to the subcommittee urging Congress not to pass the bill, saying it would limit states’ authority to regulate consumer finance. Pennsylvania, Virginia, Ohio, and Georgia did not sign.

Interchange on sales tax and tips

When a customer pays by card, the merchant’s card costs typically include an interchange fee calculated as a percentage of the full amount, including sales tax and any tip. Several states have considered excluding those portions. It is the same who-pays question as large-load tariffs, in a different market.

Illinois: from enactment to injunction

  1. June 7, 2024

    Illinois enacts the Interchange Fee Prohibition Act (HB 4951, Public Act 103-0592). It bars interchange fees on the tax and tip portions of a card transaction and limits how anyone other than the merchant may use transaction data. Original effective date: July 1, 2025.

  2. Dec. 20, 2024

    Federal district court in Chicago preliminarily enjoins the law as to national banks; extended to out-of-state state banks on Feb. 6, 2025.

  3. June 16, 2025

    Legislature delays the effective date to July 1, 2026 (HB 742, Public Act 104-0004).

  4. Feb. 10, 2026

    District court declines to permanently enjoin the fee limitation, and enjoins the data-usage limitation for federally chartered and certain other institutions.

  5. April 29, 2026

    The OCC issues an interim final rule on national bank fees and an interim final order declaring the Illinois law preempted for national banks and federal savings associations, both effective June 30.

  6. May 8, 2026

    The Seventh Circuit vacates the judgment and remands so the district court can address the OCC’s actions first.

  7. June 1, 2026

    On remand, the court permanently enjoins the fee limitation for national banks, out-of-state state banks, federal savings associations, and card networks. The same day, the legislature passes SB 3645.

  8. June 3, 2026

    Colorado’s governor vetoes SB 26-134, citing the Illinois litigation among his reasons.

  9. June 26, 2026

    Illinois’ governor signs SB 3645 (Public Act 104-0532), moving the effective date to July 1, 2027.

  10. Sept. 22, 2026

    Relying on a June NCUA rule, the court adds federal credit unions to the injunction. Illinois-chartered banks and credit unions are not among the institutions the injunction covers.

Colorado: the veto

SB 26-134 would have barred interchange on the sales-tax portion of a transaction, with an exemption for card issuers holding less than $60 billion in assets. It passed the Senate 18–17 on May 1 and the House 44–20 on May 6. Gov. Jared Polis vetoed it on June 3, 2026. His letter raised these points:

  • Legal risk. “It is quite possible this bill would never go into effect,” given the Illinois litigation and federal preemption actions.
  • Limited savings. At an average combined sales tax of about 8 percent, merchants would save roughly 0.13 percent of a purchase.
  • Feasibility. It would be “a Colorado-specific carve-out to the national and global integrated payments system.”
  • Small issuers. Community banks and credit unions told his office they would not be exempt in practice.
  • Use of savings. He objected to an amendment directing large retailers how to use the savings.

What stands out

Classification comes first

Every state law starts by deciding whether earned wage access is a loan. Most of the nine that say it is not regulate through registration or licensing, disclosure, and a required free option. The credit states regulate through lending licenses and fee caps.

Fee caps are rare; free options are common

Only Maryland, Connecticut, and Indiana cap what consumers pay. Ten of the eleven laws require at least one no-cost way to get funds, Nevada through its license application.

Tips are allowed almost everywhere, with conditions

Most states allow tips if they are disclosed as voluntary, can be zero, and do not affect service. Maryland moved from a zero-default rule to a ban, effective Oct. 1, 2026.

2026 was a year of amendments

No new state enacted an earned wage access law in 2026. Missouri moved from registration to licensing, Maryland banned tips, and Utah revised its registration rules.

On interchange, the courts are setting the pace

Illinois remains the only state with an enacted law, and federal preemption now limits who it reaches. Colorado’s governor cited the case directly, and most other 2026 bills stopped in committee.

What to watch

  • Fall 2026: House floor action on H.R. 9330; any Senate companion.
  • Fall 2026: Whether Illinois appeals the June and September injunctions. The September order refers to the parties having “re-instituted their appeal”; a new appeal is not confirmed.
  • December 31, 2026: Ohio’s 136th General Assembly ends, with SB 117 and HB 152 still in committee.
  • January 2027: New sessions in most states, including whether interchange bills held for study in New York, Rhode Island, Pennsylvania, and elsewhere return.
  • March 1, 2027: First annual earned wage access reports due to Louisiana’s Office of Financial Institutions.
  • July 1, 2027: Illinois’ interchange law takes effect for institutions not covered by the injunction, unless it changes again.
  • December 31, 2029: Nevada’s earned wage access law expires unless extended.

The question I expect to shape the next cycle: whether Congress settles the loan question for every state, or leaves it with states that have already answered it in different ways.

How this was compiled

What counts as enacted. A state is counted when its legislature has passed a statute written specifically for earned wage access. Approaches that rest only on regulation or on an attorney general opinion (Montana’s, for example) are not counted. Connecticut is counted from its 2025 act, which addressed earned wage access directly.

Sources. Each fact is drawn from a statute, enrolled bill, legislative record, regulator page, court order, Federal Register document, congressional report, or governor’s letter. Where an official site blocked automated access, the page links a text copy of the same document (LegiScan, or a law firm’s copy of a court filing or letter) and says so. Where a detail rests on a secondary source, the page says “as reported.” Where a detail could not be confirmed, it says so rather than guessing.

As of October 12, 2026. Both issues are moving. Dates on this page reflect what the sources showed on that date.
